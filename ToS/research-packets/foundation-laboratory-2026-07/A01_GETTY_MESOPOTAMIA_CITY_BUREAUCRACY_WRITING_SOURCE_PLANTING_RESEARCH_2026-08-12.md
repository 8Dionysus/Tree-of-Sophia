### Required decomposition of the overview

At minimum the source's compressed scene must be represented through separate
variables:

1. settlement extent, density, morphology, and population estimate;
2. monumental, public, domestic, industrial, and ritual architecture;
3. irrigation works, agricultural production, and maintenance coordination;
4. storage, allocation, redistribution, labor, and craft specialization;
5. administrative artifacts: tokens, sealings, bullae, numerical tablets,
   lexical tablets, and other records;
6. inferred institutions and actors: household, neighborhood, temple, palace,
   office, elite, craft group, and wider polity;
7. notation's functional coverage and relation to language;
8. chronology, provenience, excavation context, sample survival, and dating
   model;
9. regional interaction, mobility, local adoption, resistance, and hybrid
   practice.

`City`, `urbanization`, `bureaucracy`, `administration`, `accounting`,
`writing`, `proto-writing`, and `authority` are not synonyms. A source may
observe material traces while its inference about an institution remains
model-dependent. Co-occurrence is not causation; a date range is not a
mechanism; a modern analytical label is not an ancient self-description.

## 2. Established top scholarship

### Nissen, Damerow, and Englund: writing and economic administration

The exact CDLI publication record for Hans J. Nissen, Peter Damerow, and
Robert K. Englund's
[*Archaic Bookkeeping: Writing and Techniques of Economic Administration in
the Ancient Near East*](https://cdli.earth/publications/1657148) identifies
the 1993 Work. It remains the principal established synthesis for the relation
between archaic writing and techniques of economic administration.

Its relevance is strong but bounded. A corpus dominated by administrative
records can establish functions, categories, and techniques without proving
that every city institution belonged to one centralized bureaucracy, that
temples monopolized administration, or that writing caused urbanization.
`Economic administration` is a source- and corpus-bounded role, not a license
for one universal graph edge.

### Algaze: an influential urban-landscape model, not settled consensus

Guillermo Algaze's
[*Ancient Mesopotamia at the Dawn of Civilization: The Evolution of an Urban
Landscape*](https://academic.oup.com/chicago-scholarship-online/book/303),
DOI `10.7208/chicago/9780226013787.001.0001`, was published by the
University of Chicago Press in 2008. It is an influential synthesis of early
urbanism, economic differentiation, and regional interaction.

For ToS this is a model-bearing Work. Its arguments and variables deserve
explicit identity, but later local-agency and regional evidence can contest
its top-down implications. Influence does not make every element consensus,
and a museum overview cannot cite the model as if it were a direct artifact
observation.

### Damerow: proto-writing is not silently full language representation

Peter Damerow's
[“The Origins of Writing as a Problem of Historical Epistemology”](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlj/2006-1),
*Cuneiform Digital Library Journal* 2006:1, supplies the established writing
control. It treats proto-cuneiform as restricted administrative notation with
limited semantic categories, incomplete conventionalization, and weak or
absent phonetic coding, followed by structural reorganization toward oral
language.

The Getty phrase `earliest writing` can therefore orient a reader but cannot
settle the analytical status of every early tablet. ToS must preserve which
definition of writing a claim uses and which evidence makes that definition
applicable.

### Foundation rule from the established literature

The stable foundation is not a single immutable interpretation of ancient
urban life. It is the returnable identity of sites, contexts, artifacts,
representations, passages, scholarly Works, claims, definitions, and models,
plus explicit uncertainty and supersession. On that foundation, rival causal
accounts can coexist without corrupting the source layer.

## 3. Fresh and current relevance check

Freshness was applied after official documentation and established work. A
2026 date did not outweigh a 2025 paper when the older paper tested the exact
claim more directly.

### Lewis 2025: local agency instead of a one-way Uruk expansion

Michael P. Lewis's
[“Reframing the Uruk Expansion: Glocalization and Local Dynamics in the Late
Chalcolithic Adhaim-Sirwan Drainage Basin, Iraqi Kurdistan”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/reframing-the-uruk-expansion-glocalization-and-local-dynamics-in-the-late-chalcolithic-adhaimsirwan-drainage-basin-iraqi-kurdistan/6D0017216B86F0F5084F8BD4C2504A9A),
DOI `10.1017/S0959774324000404`, was published online 26 March 2025
and in *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 35(2). It is CC BY 4.0; no payload
was imported.

Lewis replaces top-down grand narrative with local agency, selective adoption,
coexistence, and glocalized interaction. Administrative evidence in the study
region is rare; a numerical tablet, sealings, or tokens provide bounded traces
whose institutional interpretation requires context. Southern material does
not automatically prove a southern colony, centralized bureaucracy, or
wholesale cultural replacement.

This directly constrains any graph derived from the Getty overview:
`southern-style object present`, `southern person present`, `local practice
changed`, `settlement administratively subordinated`, and `urban system
caused from Uruk` must remain different claims.

### Fragnoli and colleagues 2025: northern urbanization and multiple paths

P. Fragnoli, R. Crocco, J. H. Sterba, F. Balossi Restelli, and colleagues'
[“Pottery production, early urbanization and the Uruk phenomenon at Tell Brak
in Upper Mesopotamia”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12520-025-02290-8),
DOI `10.1007/s12520-025-02290-8`, was published 5 August 2025 in
*Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences* 17:178. It is CC BY 4.0, with
the normal separate-credit caveat for third-party material; no payload or
supplement entered ToS.

The paper situates proto-urban growth and social complexity at Tell Brak in
the late fifth and early fourth millennia, before the strongest Late Uruk
southern material presence. It separates local production, coordinated raw
material procurement, craft specialization, redistribution, long-distance
exchange, elite material culture, neighborhood aggregation, and later
southern interaction.

This is a strong current counterexample to the idea that all key elements of
urban civilization first appeared together in one southern center or followed
one institutional sequence. It does not prove the opposite universal model;
it requires ToS to represent multiple regional trajectories.

### Khanipour and Jamshidi Yeganeh 2025: sealing without city or writing

Morteza Khanipour and Sepideh Jamshidi Yeganeh's
[“New evidence for sealing in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic from Tol-e Sangi,
Iran”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/new-evidence-for-sealing-in-the-prepottery-neolithic-from-tole-sangi-iran/38BB1B87394ADC75B5CFF82498B947A2),
DOI `10.15184/aqy.2024.234`, was published online 16 January 2025 and
in *Antiquity* 99(405), June 2025, e17. It is CC BY 4.0; no article text or
figure was imported.

The Pre-Pottery Neolithic context contains a sealing and small objects that
may be tokens. This makes a direct conceptual control: storage control,
sealing, tokens, accounting, complex hierarchy, urbanism, bureaucracy, and
writing cannot be inferred from one another by object class alone. Even the
function of a small clay object depends on context and competing
interpretations.

### Yousefi Zoshk and Hessari 2026: writing over sealings as experiment

Rouhollah Yousefi Zoshk and Morteza Hessari's
[“Script over Sealings: Proto-Elamite Bureaucratic Innovation at Tepe
Sofalin, Iran”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/739810), DOI
`10.1086/739810`, appears in *Near Eastern Archaeology* 89(1), March 2026.
The publisher page exposes public metadata and an abstract but marks the full
article `No Access`, credits copyright to the American Society of Overseas
Research, and reserves text-and-data-mining and AI-training rights. No full
text or PDF was acquired or analyzed.

The public abstract treats three Proto-Elamite sealings bearing written signs
as evidence for a shift or experiment from seal-based to combined
writing-and-sealing authority. It explicitly keeps sign interpretation,
centralized-oversight implications, and local adaptation open.

The fresh value is methodological: the relation between writing and sealing
can be an experiment in administrative strategy, not a universal replacement
stage. `inscribed sealing`, `textual accountability`, `central oversight`,
`elite authority`, and `local adaptation` need separate evidence.

### Current but not closest: Khosravi 2026

Shokouh Khosravi's
[“The late prehistoric administrative artefacts from Tapeh Tyalineh,
Kermanshah, western Iran”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/late-prehistoric-administrative-artefacts-from-tapeh-tyalineh-kermanshah-western-iran/7375FF97E8C9D411B4EF28244E212D13),
DOI `10.15184/aqy.2025.10259`, was published online 6 January 2026. It
is a valuable future geographic and administrative-technology control, but
its early-third-millennium context is less direct for the Late Uruk bundle.
It is deferred rather than selected merely because it is newer.

### Bounded currentness conclusion

The search located no 2026 full synthesis that cleanly supersedes the combined
established and 2025 direct evidence for this exact city-bureaucracy-writing
question. The 2026 Tepe Sofalin study is narrower and useful precisely because
it exposes an experimental relation. This is a bounded search result as of 12
August 2026, not proof that no other relevant work exists.

Generic first-city summaries, causal retellings, social posts, and popular
claims that temples or bureaucracy simply invented writing were rejected.
They restate the proposition instead of testing it.

## Laboratory consequences

The future A/B/C study must test epistemic behavior, not only schema validity:

- **A — museum-synthesis decomposition:** freeze one permitted Getty revision
  and split it into atomic propositions. Label observation, definition,
  chronology, population estimate, institutional inference, co-occurrence,
  mechanism, and causal direction separately.
- **B — direct-evidence matrix:** for every A proposition, build a
  source-returnable matrix across site, phase, stratum, object type, material
  context, measurement, scholarly model, and alternative interpretation.
  Established scholarship and artifact-level evidence replace fluent
  compression.
- **C — currentness and counterexample challenge:** confront B with northern
  urbanization, local-agency models, non-written sealing/storage control, and
  combined writing-sealing experiments. Retain alternatives instead of
  forcing one winning story where evidence does not decide.

Primary measures are:

1. number of unsupported causal or equivalence edges;
2. returnability from every proposition to exact source and evidence layer;
3. preservation of competing models and negative evidence;
4. separation of observable artifact from institutional inference;
5. rights compliance and source-payload containment;
6. elapsed time, machine time, local compute cost, and storage cost;
7. amount of consequential human judgment required.

Schema validators can prove structural consistency but cannot prove historical
truth. Manual source return, counterexample search, and adversarial reading are
decisive. Human work remains reserved for consequential promotion or unresolved
conflict, not routine transcription or repeated UI exercises.

No A/B/C run is executed in this planting.
