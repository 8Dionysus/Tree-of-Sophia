```text

```

The exact dossier citation `[56]` resolves to the mutable Wikipedia article
[*Eduba*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduba). It is retained as the
historical discovery lead that motivated the control, not as the planted
authority. The strongest current standalone overview located is Jana

cuneiform use, while detailed local curricula can mainly be reconstructed for

The freshest direct correction is Eleanor Robson's chapter “Back to House F:
on community, temporality and locality in Old Babylonian scribal schooling”
in the open-access June 2026 volume
[*Scribal Worlds*](https://uclpress.co.uk/book/scribal-worlds/). Robson warns
that preliminary models have become reified as historical truth and argues
for informality, variability, locality, and historically sensitive use of
“school” and “curriculum”. This fresh result strengthens the fence; it does
not create a second bibliographic Work in this bounded A01 planting because
the chapter belongs to an aggregate volume whose contained-Work and
Collection identities must later be modeled together.

## What the source is controlling

A01 concerns Late Uruk proto-cuneiform accounting and lexical material. The
later afterlife of lists matters because copying and curricular reuse can
preserve forms while changing the function, language, ordering, medium,
institution, and intended audience. The control therefore prevents three
collapses:

1. **chronological collapse** — treating an Old Babylonian institution of the
   early second millennium BCE as the social setting of writing at Late Uruk;
2. **documentary collapse** — treating a modern scholarly composite, one
   physical tablet, an exercise, a catalogue, a literary composition, and an
   institution as the same evidence object;
3. **functional collapse** — treating formal resemblance or membership in a
   long-lived lexical tradition as proof that a list served the same purpose
   across periods and places.

The A01 dossier itself already separates formation of the Late Uruk node,
roughly 3400–3000 BCE, from a later school and lexicographic afterlife. The
present research makes that separation operational.

## 1. Classical documentation and source-returnable routes

### DCCLT: lexical tradition changes role

The official ORACC
[*Digital Corpus of Cuneiform Lexical Texts: Introduction*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/intro/lexical_intro.html)
provides the clearest documentary bridge. It distinguishes archaic thematic
lists from a newly organized Old Babylonian lexical curriculum around 1900
BCE and from later scholarly or hermeneutic use. The continuity is therefore
a history of a changing textual tradition, not one invariant institutional
edge.

For ToS, a continuity claim must separately test at least:

- composition or list-family identity;
- exact entries and order;
- sign forms and readings;
- language and bilingual columns;
- tablet format and writing hand;
- find context and date;
- pedagogical, administrative, archival, or scholarly function;
- local transmission, copying, recomposition, and variants.

### OBMC: Old Babylonian evidence is local and domestic

The official ORACC
[*Old Babylonian School*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/obmc/OldBabylonianSchool/index.html)
overview places the richest evidence in the Old Babylonian period and names
Nippur, Ur, Isin, Sippar, and Kish. The evidence commonly comes from private
houses. “School” is a convenient analytical label, not proof of one central,
regulated institution shared everywhere.

### ePSD2: term occurrence is not an institution identity

The ePSD2 route records `edubbaʾa [scribal school]`, written `e2-dub-ba-a`:

- <https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dcclt/signlists/cbd/sux/E.html>

The lexical record supports the existence and reading of the term in its
attested contexts. It does not license adding an `edubba` organization to
Late Uruk or assigning the word to any tablet that lacks an exact occurrence.
Ancient spelling, normalized lexeme, modern translation, context-specific
sense, and modern institution class remain separate.

### CDLI: composite and witnesses remain distinct

CDLI exposes
[*CDLI Literary 000754 (Edubba A) composite*](https://cdli.earth/P464238)
as an Old Babylonian Sumerian scholarly composite associated with Nippur. It
is not one ancient physical tablet. A future ToS route must distinguish:

```text
composition identity
  -> modern scholarly composite
  -> member or witness claims
  -> physical artifacts
  -> representations and transcriptions
```

The literary composition is evidence for how school life was represented. It
is not transparent ethnography of every Old Babylonian teaching setting.

### Penn Museum: preservation and exercise bias

Steve Tinney's official Penn Museum article
[*Texts, Tablets, and Teaching: Scribal Education in Nippur and Ur*](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/texts-tablets-and-teaching/)
was originally published in *Expedition* 40(2), July 1998. It documents the
sequence of exercises, tablet types, Nippur/Ur differences, recycling, and
archaeological accident. It also makes an essential source-critical point:
student writing was an exercise, not necessarily a superior master copy made
to preserve literature for posterity.

Thus absence, survival count, tablet type, error, and variant cannot be read
without the formation process of the assemblage.

## 2. Established and field-shaping scholarship

### Tinney 1999: a reconstructable curricular sequence, not a universal law

Steve Tinney,
[*On the Curricular Setting of Sumerian Literature*](https://doi.org/10.2307/4200472),
*Iraq* 61 (1999): 159–172, reconstructs parts of the Old Babylonian Nippur
literary curriculum from catalogues, tablet types, and textual groupings. It
is a high-value model of source-bounded reconstruction. Its Nippur focus and
the provisional character of some sequences prohibit universalization.

### Robson 2001: House F as archaeological control

Eleanor Robson,
[*The Tablet House: A Scribal School in Old Babylonian Nippur*](https://doi.org/10.3917/assy.093.0039),
*Revue d'assyriologie et d'archéologie orientale* 93 (2001): 39–66,
reconstructs House F through an assemblage of roughly 1,400 fragments and
tests literary pictures of school against archaeological evidence. The
article is foundational precisely because it does not permit an idealized
edubba text to substitute for the house, tablets, stratigraphy, or people.

### Veldhuis 2014: long duration with changing functions

Niek C. Veldhuis,
[*History of the Cuneiform Lexical Tradition*](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/history-of-the-cuneiform-lexical-tradition/),
Guides to the Mesopotamian Textual Record 6 (Ugarit-Verlag, 2014), is the
field-scale control for the long history of lexical texts. The University of
Helsinki research record identifies it as a peer-reviewed scientific book.
It supports modeling a tradition across time while preserving the difference
between technical documentation, pedagogical sequence, reference work, and
hermeneutics.

### Mature conclusion

The established literature permits claims such as “this later witness belongs
to a transmitted list tradition” only when the exact basis is stated. It does
not permit the stronger shortcuts “the same school existed”, “the same
curriculum persisted”, or “the list meant the same thing”.

## 3. Fresh current relevance check

### Oxford 2025: the selected current standalone overview

Jana Matuszak's
[*education, Mesopotamian*](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.9149)
was published by Oxford University Press on 17 December 2025 as a reference
entry in the evolving Oxford Classical Dictionary. Its accessible abstract
states that evidence for education is unevenly distributed and that detailed
local curricula can mainly be reconstructed for Babylonia in the second and
first millennia BCE. This is the exact current proposition needed by A01.

The full entry is access-controlled. ToS creates only a verified Work identity

### Robson 2026: current correction of reified models

UCL Press published
[*Scribal Worlds: Scholarship and classification in cuneiform cultures*](https://uclpress.co.uk/book/scribal-worlds/)
on 29 June 2026, DOI `10.14324/111.9781806550821`, under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.
Chapter 7, Eleanor Robson's “Back to House F: on community, temporality and
locality in Old Babylonian scribal schooling”, pp. 125–151, is the freshest
direct source found before the 2026-08-12 cut-off.

The chapter explicitly reopens preliminary findings that became reified as
historical truth. Its conclusion asks researchers to set aside assumptions of
formal institutions, regulation, and standardization; “school” and
“curriculum” remain useful only with historical sensitivity. Community,
temporality, locality, irregular participation, recycling, dispersed finds,
and the distinction between customs and rules become evidence dimensions.

The official open PDF was inspected transiently from the UCL repository to
verify the chapter and pages. It was not copied into ToS, committed, or made a
source Item. Temporary verification bytes are not a ToS acquisition.

### ISAC 2023: current museum and assemblage synthesis

The official ISAC catalog
[*Back to School in Babylonia*](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/ISACMP/isacmp1),
edited by Susanne Paulus with Marta Díaz Herrera, Jane Gordon, Madeline
Ouimet, Colton G. Siegmund, and Ryan D. Winters, was published in 2023. It
contains 20 essays and a 126-object catalog and describes House F as widely
believed to have been a scribal school. That careful wording is evidence of a
live classification question, not hesitation to be erased by a generated
graph.

### ScriPTS 2024: variation defeats a single curriculum projection

The European Commission CORDIS report for
[*Scribal Performance and Textual Standardization in Mesopotamian Lexical Lists*](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101063660/reporting),
project `101063660`, DOI `10.3030/101063660`, records a 2022–2024 closed
research project. Its report describes local and communal variation in
reading and textual traditions and explicitly moves beyond a uniform view of
cuneiform education. It is a project report, not a substitute for its final
peer-reviewed outputs; its status remains explicit.
