### What the brief synthesis may and may not do

The Britannica page is useful as a conventional entry point: it supplies a
broad term under which readers expect wedge-shaped writing of the ancient
Middle East and a familiar high-level origin narrative. That is a query seed,
not a final ontology.

For ToS, every apparently simple sentence must be decomposed. At minimum these
questions remain independent:

1. what counts as `writing` rather than `proto-writing` in the cited model;
2. which archaeological phase, site, stratum, tablet group, and dating model
   support an `earliest` claim;
3. whether `Sumerian` names a later linguistic reading, a population label,
   a scholarly attribution, or source-visible phonetic evidence;
4. whether a proposed sign precursor is a token, seal motif, pictorial source,
   numerical notation, or later retrospective similarity;
5. whether a sign has a stable denotation, a context-bound administrative
   role, a reading, a phonetic value, or only a modern catalog label;
6. whether `cuneiform` is being used narrowly for wedge-impressed script or
   broadly for preceding proto-cuneiform forms.

Britannica cannot close these distinctions by compression.

## 2. Established top scholarship

### Damerow: origin as a historical-epistemic problem

Peter Damerow's [“The Origins of Writing as a Problem of Historical
Epistemology”](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlj/2006-1), *Cuneiform Digital
Library Journal* 2006:1, is the principal established control. Its model
describes proto-cuneiform as restricted administrative notation with simple
semantic categories, weak or absent phonetic coding, incomplete
conventionalization, spatial hierarchies, bookkeeping, and lexical lists. It
places the system in continuity with older symbolic administration while
arguing that later adaptation to oral language reorganized both structure and
use.

The methodological consequence is crucial: the path is not a universal linear
sequence from pictures to words to phonetic writing. Different tracks and
internal reorganizations must remain expressible. A broad encyclopedia origin
story cannot be projected into a single `developed_from` edge.

### Woods: visible and permanent language, retained as a competing frame

The official Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures publication record
for [*Visible Language: Inventions of Writing in the Ancient Middle East and
Beyond*](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/oimp/oimp-32-visible-language-inventions-writing-ancient-middle-east-and)
identifies Christopher Woods as editor, with Emily Teeter and Geoff Emberling,
and distinguishes the 2015 reprint with minor corrections from the 2010 first
edition. Woods's chapters frame writing through the ability to make language
visible and permanent and synthesize the earliest Mesopotamian evidence.

That definition is useful, but it cannot silently settle the status of
proto-cuneiform against Damerow's account of a system still far from oral
language. ToS must retain the definition, its scope, and the evidence under
which a source applies it. `Writing`, `proto-writing`, `notation`, and
`language representation` are claim-bearing analytical categories, not
universal metadata values.

### Foundation rule from the established literature

The stable foundation is not one immutable meaning assigned to every ancient
sign. It is the source-returnable identity of artifacts, representations,
passages, scholarly Works, and claims, plus explicit uncertainty and
supersession. On that foundation, competing interpretations can coexist
without corrupting the source layer.

## 3. Fresh and current relevance check

### Kelley, Cartolano, and Ferrara: multimedia precursors, not shape alone

Kathryn Kelley, Mattia Cartolano, and Silvia Ferrara's
[“Seals and signs: tracing the origins of writing in ancient South-west
Asia”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804),
DOI `10.15184/aqy.2024.165`, was published online 5 November 2024 and in
*Antiquity* 99(403), February 2025, pages 64–82. The online date and issue date
remain distinct. The article is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0; no text or figure is imported.

The article is the freshest directly relevant origin study located in the
bounded search. It treats proto-cuneiform, attested at Uruk around 3350–3000
BCE, as a complex accounting system with hundreds of still partly
uninterpreted iconographic signs. It emphasizes that token-to-sign ancestry
outside numerals is rarely demonstrable and that sign origins are diverse and
multimedia. Context-supported correlations can be proposed between selected
pre-literate seal motifs and later signs, while seals and writing remain
independent systems and many seal chronologies remain insecure.

This is a direct current correction to shape-only genealogy. Visual
resemblance is a candidate route; context, chronology, medium, use, and
independence tests determine whether it can become a claim.

### Unicode 17: current technical terminology, not archaeological authority

The final [Unicode Standard 17.0, chapter 11](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-11/)
is a useful 2026 technical control. It distinguishes the late-fourth-
millennium ideographic stage from cuneiform proper while noting that
`cuneiform` is often used broadly for earlier forms, and it describes the
later Sumero-Akkadian system as logographic with a strong syllabic component.

Unicode determines character encoding behavior, not the truth of every
archaeological or linguistic origin claim. Its description of the mature
system must not be projected backward onto proto-cuneiform. It belongs in the
terminology and interoperability layer.

### 2025–2026 currentness screen

A final targeted search of *Antiquity*, CDLI journals, and DOI-indexed results
found no 2025–2026 peer-reviewed full resynthesis that supersedes the direct
origin problem addressed by Kelley, Cartolano, and Ferrara. Current CDLI 2025
and 2026 articles located in the search concern later texts, objects, visual
legibility, or numerical material rather than a complete reconstruction of
proto-cuneiform origins. Generic cuneiform OCR and sign-classification work
was rejected as non-responsive to this backlog anchor.

This is a bounded search result as of 12 August 2026, not a claim that no such
work exists.

## Laboratory consequences

The smallest honest A/B/C design for a future permitted revision is:

- **A — synthesis baseline:** use the encyclopedia article only to generate a
  list of atomic propositions and search routes. Record every proposition's
  exact revision anchor and whether it is definition, chronology, attribution,
  mechanism, or genealogy.
- **B — direct-source decomposition:** replace each compressed proposition
  with source-returnable established scholarship and, where possible,
  artifact-level evidence. Preserve disagreements and measure unsupported
  assumptions rather than rewarding fluent agreement.
- **C — currentness challenge:** test B against the freshest directly relevant
  studies and current technical standards. A new date alone does not outrank a
  stronger source; a fresh result must address the same claim and evidence
  layer.

Primary measures are returnability to exact evidence, number of unsupported
assertions, preserved competing interpretations, rights compliance, elapsed
time, machine cost, and the amount of consequential human judgment needed.
Prepared validators can check schema shape but cannot declare the experiment
epistemically correct. Manual source return and counterexample review remain
the decisive checks.

Human work is reserved for promotion or unresolved consequential conflict,
not routine page transcription. No A/B/C run is executed in this planting.
