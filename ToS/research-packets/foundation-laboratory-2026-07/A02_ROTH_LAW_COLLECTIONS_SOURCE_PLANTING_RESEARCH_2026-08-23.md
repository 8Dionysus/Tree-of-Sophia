## 2. Established field-shaping scholarship

Bernard M. Levinson's 2000 *Journal of Near Eastern Studies* [review](https://doi.org/10.1086/468800) characterizes the second edition as both a comprehensive translation/reference work and, in substance, a new critical edition built from systematic analysis of tablets and prior editions. His review is especially important for ToS because it identifies the internal scholarly layers that a bare `translation` label would hide:

- collection-specific introductions;
- interpretation and source history;
- variants and restorations;
- textual divisions and paragraph numbering;
- translation notes;
- normalized Akkadian alongside English rather than a purely diplomatic witness transcription;
- separate Hoffner responsibility for the Hittite section.

Therefore a future Roth-backed quotation or translation alignment must cite not only the Work but the exact edition, collection, page/section, language presentation, and responsibility layer. A normalized form is already an editorial interpretation; it cannot silently become a diplomatic transcription or an original sign sequence.

Amélie Kuhrt's 1997 *American Journal of Legal History* [review record](https://doi.org/10.2307/845485) and the publisher's retained review notices establish early reception. They are reception evidence, not independent verification of each reading or translation.

The 2023 SBL volume [*Judicial Decisions in the Ancient Near East*](https://cart.sbl-site.org/books/061559E), edited by Sophie Démare-Lafont and Daniel E. Fleming, is a crucial current field-shaping comparison. It treats records and letters from legal practice as a different corpus and explicitly positions the new volume beside Roth's legal-collection classic. This supports complementarity, not supersession:

```text
law collections and scholarly formulations
  ≠ records of judicial decisions and practical application
```

For ToS this means Roth may ground a comparative translation/control route while contracts, court cases, letters, and administrative practice retain separate Work, genre, witness, and evidential identities.
